Comparing things, from bigger to far greater

The new model is considerably more efficient than the old one, and nowhere near as noisy.

At B2 you can already say bigger and more expensive. The step up is control: saying how much bigger, saying when things are equal or almost equal, and choosing the right word, because big, large, great and high are not interchangeable.

The forms, quickly

You probably know these already. Use the table as a quick check, then look carefully at the common mistakes below it.

TypeComparativeSuperlative
One syllablecheap → cheaper; big → biggerthe cheapest; the biggest
Two syllables ending in -yeasy → easier; busy → busierthe easiest; the busiest
Two or more syllablesmodern → more modern; expensive → more expensivethe most modern; the most expensive
Either formquiet, simple, narrow, clever: quieter or more quietthe quietest or the most quiet
Irregulargood → better; bad → worse; far → further / fartherthe best; the worst; the furthest / farthest
Quantity wordsmuch / many → more; little → lessthe most; the least
Further or farther? Both work for distance. Only further means additional: further information, further questions.

Mistakes that even advanced speakers make

Not thisBut this
incorrect: more easier, incorrect: more bettereasier, better
incorrect: the most biggestthe biggest
incorrect: very bigger, incorrect: too cheapermuch bigger, far cheaper
incorrect: superior than, incorrect: inferior thansuperior to, inferior to

Than me or than I am? He’s taller than me is normal in speech. He’s taller than I am is a little more careful. He’s taller incorrect: than I sounds stiff and old-fashioned.
Different from, to or than? Different from is safe everywhere. Different to is common in the UK and Ireland; different than is common in the US.

Check yourself

  1. This route is (short) than the motorway.

    One syllable: add -er.

  2. The second interview went (bad) than the first.

    Bad is irregular: bad, worse, the worst.

  3. That was the (easy) exam I’ve ever taken.

    Two syllables ending in -y: change y to i and add -est.

  4. For (far) information, contact reception.

    Only further means additional.

  5. This version is blank than the last one.

    Use much (not very) to make a comparative stronger, and never add more to better.

  6. Our product is superior blank theirs.

    Superior and inferior take to.

  7. There were blank people at the conference than last year.

    People is countable, so careful English uses fewer.

How much bigger?

This is where B2 becomes C1. Instead of just cheaper, say how much cheaper. The words below go directly before the comparative.

  • no difference at all
    no + comparative, not any + comparative: The new version is no faster than the old one. It isn’t any cheaper either.
  • a tiny difference
    marginally (formal), slightly, a little, a bit (informal): Costs are slightly higher this year.
  • a moderate difference
    somewhat (formal), noticeably: The second draft is noticeably clearer.
  • a big difference
    much, far, a lot (informal), considerably, significantly, substantially (formal): The new system is far more reliable.
Even. Use even when the difference is bigger than people expect: It was expensive last year, and it’s even more expensive now.
Much more or many more? Use many more with plural countable nouns and much more with uncountable nouns: We need many more volunteers and much more time.
Informal speech. You’ll often hear way for a big difference: It’s way better than I expected. Keep it out of formal writing.

Check yourself

  1. Profits were only blank higher than expected, about one percent.

    A one percent difference is small: slightly or marginally.

  2. We need blank more staff to cover the summer.

    Staff here means people, so many more.

  3. The expensive model is blank better than the cheap one. They’re basically identical.

    No + comparative means no difference at all.

  4. Rent is blank more expensive than it was five years ago. It has almost doubled.

    Almost double is a big difference: significantly, considerably or far.

  5. The first film was scary, but the sequel is blank scarier.

    Even shows the difference is bigger than expected.

Equal, almost equal, nowhere near

Comparison isn’t only about one thing being bigger. Often the point is that two things are the same, or nearly the same.

PatternExample
as … asThe train is as fast as the plane, door to door.
just as … asOnline meetings can be just as productive as in-person ones.
not as / not so … asThis year wasn’t as busy as last year.
almost / nearly as … asShe’s nearly as tall as her brother.
not quite as … asThe remake is not quite as good as the original.
nowhere near as … asThe sequel is nowhere near as funny as the first film.
twice / three times as … asThe new battery lasts twice as long as the old one.
half as much / many … asWe spent half as much as we had planned.
as many / much + noun + asWe don’t have as many orders as last year.
the same (+ noun) asIt’s the same size as the old one. She earns the same as me.
similar toYour situation is similar to mine.
equally + adjectiveBoth candidates are equally qualified.
Three times bigger? Three times bigger than is very common in speech and journalism. Three times as big as is the more careful form.

Check yourself

  1. The new office is blank the old one, about 200 square metres each.

    The pattern is the same + noun + as.

  2. The copy is good, but it’s blank the original.

    After as … as, use the base adjective and as, never than.

  3. This laptop cost twice as as my old one.

    Twice as much as compares amounts of money or other uncountable things.

  4. Your experience is very similar mine.

    Similar takes to.

  5. The remake is nowhere as funny as the original.

    Nowhere near as … as means very far from equal.

Big, large, great or high?

These four words all suggest more, but they combine with different nouns. Choosing the right one is one of the clearest signs of a C1 speaker.

WordUsed forTypical combinations
biggeneral size or importance; conversationala big house, a big problem, a big decision, a bigger office
largesize, amounts and numbers; more formala large number of, a large amount of, a large proportion, a large company
greatabstract nouns; degree or intensitygreat importance, great care, great detail, greater flexibility, greater demand, greater risk
highlevels, rates, prices and positions on a scalehigh prices, high costs, a high salary, high interest rates, a high level of, high quality

Some nouns prefer other words entirely:

WordTypical combinations
widea wider range, a wider choice, wider support
strongstronger demand, a stronger position, a strong argument
heavyheavier traffic, heavy losses, heavy rain
deepa deeper understanding, deep concern
The opposites follow the same pattern. lower prices, a smaller number, less interest, fewer options, a narrower range, weaker demand.

Check yourself

  1. It took a blank amount of time to train the new staff.

    Amounts and numbers are large.

  2. The job comes with a blank salary.

    Salaries, prices and rates are high.

  3. The new contract gives managers blank flexibility.

    Abstract nouns like flexibility, importance and risk take great.

  4. There’s a blank range of options than last year.

    A range or choice is wide.

  5. Demand is much blank in winter.

    Levels and demand are high (also: greater or stronger).

  6. The report covers the issue in blank detail.

    The fixed combination is in great detail.

  7. Traffic is much blank on Friday afternoons.

    Traffic is heavy or light.

Patterns that compare

The more…, the more…

Two comparatives linked together show that one change causes another. Each half starts with the.

  • The sooner we start, the sooner we’ll finish.
  • The more I read about it, the less I understand.
  • Short form: The sooner, the better. The bigger, the better.

Getting bigger and bigger

Repeat the comparative to show gradual change.

  • It’s getting harder and harder to find a flat.
  • Flights are becoming more and more expensive, not incorrect: more expensive and more expensive.

Preferring one thing to another

  • I prefer tea to coffee. I prefer walking to driving.
  • I’d rather walk than take the bus.
  • I’d prefer to walk rather than take the bus.

Superlatives in use

  • by far the most popular option
  • one of the best restaurants in town (always a plural noun after one of the)
  • the second largest city in the country
  • the least expensive option
  • the best film I’ve ever seen (present perfect after a superlative)
  • the tallest building in the city (use in for places and groups); the best day of my life (use of for periods and numbers)

Check yourself

  1. The you practise, the easier it gets.

    The pattern is the + comparative, the + comparative.

  2. It’s getting colder and every day.

    Repeat the comparative to show gradual change.

  3. This is one of the best (restaurant) in town.

    One of the is always followed by a plural noun.

  4. It’s by the most popular option.

    By far makes a superlative stronger.

  5. I prefer working from home working in the office.

    Prefer X to Y.

  6. She’s the most experienced person the team.

    Use in with groups and places.

  7. It was the best decision I’ve made.

    The best … I’ve ever…

Comparing ideas in speech and writing

Beyond -er and more, these words and phrases let you compare whole situations. Most are useful in meetings, reports and presentations.

ExpressionExample
compared to / withCompared to last year, sales are up 8%.
in comparison (with)In comparison, the older model feels slow.
by comparisonTrains are expensive. Buses are cheap by comparison.
relative toWages have fallen relative to inflation.
whereas / whileTeam A works remotely, whereas Team B is office-based.
unlikeUnlike its rivals, the company has no debt.
similarly / likewisePrices rose in Spain. Similarly, Italy saw an increase.
on a par withTheir quality is now on a par with ours.
outperform / exceed / surpassDemand exceeded expectations. The new team outperformed every other region.
lag behind / fall short ofWe’re lagging behind our competitors. Results fell short of the target.
in line withGrowth was in line with forecasts.
a far cry fromThe flat was a far cry from the one in the photos.

Check yourself

  1. Results were exactly as predicted, blank forecasts.

    In line with means matching.

  2. blank most of its rivals, the company is still growing.

    Unlike is followed by a noun. Whereas needs a full clause.

  3. Sales of the new model blank all expectations.

    Exceed expectations, a target, a limit.

  4. My first job paid very little. My current salary is blank that.

    A far cry from means very different from.

  5. The north of the country is wet, blank the south is very dry.

    Whereas joins two full clauses that contrast.
