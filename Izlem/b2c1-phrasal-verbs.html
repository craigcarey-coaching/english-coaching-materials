Phrasal verbs: one verb, many meanings

Can we put off the meeting? I still need to look into the figures.

Learn phrasal verbs in two directions: the high-frequency ones you’ll hear every day, and the families that grow from a single verb such as get, put or take. Then learn when a single, more formal verb is the better choice.

How phrasal verbs work

A phrasal verb is a verb plus a particle (a small word such as up, out, off or on). Sometimes the meaning is literal: sit down, go out. More often it is new: put off means postpone, turn up means arrive. Four facts make them much easier to use.

  • Many can split. With a noun object, the particle can go before or after it: turn down the offer or turn the offer down. With a pronoun (it, them, her), the particle must go after: turn it down, never incorrect: turn down it.
  • Some never split. Verbs followed by a preposition keep the object after the particle: look after the children, look after them, never incorrect: look them after. The same applies to deal with, come across, run into, get over.
  • Three-part verbs never split. look forward to, put up with, come up with, run out of, get on with, catch up on. In look forward to, the to is a preposition, so it takes a noun or an -ing form: I look forward to hearing from you, not incorrect: to hear.
  • Stress helps you hear them. In speech, the particle is usually stressed: turn down, give up, set it up. With a verb plus a preposition, the verb is stressed and the preposition is weak: look at it, deal with it. Listening for that stress helps you catch phrasal verbs in fast speech.

Check yourself

  1. That’s a great offer. Why did you blank?

    With a pronoun, the particle goes after it: turn it down.

  2. The children are at home alone. Who’s blank?

    Look after never splits: look after them.

  3. I’m looking forward to blank you next week.

    In look forward to, the to is a preposition, so an -ing form follows.

  4. We’ve blank milk. Can you buy some on the way home?

    Three-part verbs keep all their parts together: run out of something.

  5. I can’t blank this noise much longer.

    Put up with (tolerate) is a three-part verb and never splits.

What the particles tend to mean

Particles are not random. Each has a few typical meanings that repeat across many verbs. These are tendencies, not rules, but they help you make a good guess when you meet a phrasal verb for the first time.

up

completion; increase; appearing

use up, eat up, speed up, turn up, come up

out

completely, to the end; discovering; from inside to outside

sort out, figure out, find out, run out, point out

off

cancelling; separating; leaving; starting

call off, cut off, set off, kick off

down

reducing; stopping; recording on paper

cut down, slow down, turn down, write down, break down

over

reviewing; passing control to someone

go over, look over, think over, take over, hand over

back

returning; replying; reducing

pay back, call back, get back to, cut back

on

continuing; accepting

carry on, keep on, go on, take on

through

from start to finish

get through, go through, follow through, see it through

Check yourself

Use the meanings above to choose the particle.

  1. Please carry with your work. I’ll only be a minute.

    On often means continuing: carry on, keep on, go on.

  2. Prices keep rising, so we’ve cut on travel.

    Both cut back on and cut down on mean reduce.

  3. Let me think it and I’ll call you tomorrow.

    Over often means reviewing: think over, go over, look over.

  4. We’ve used all the paper. Can you order more?

    Up often means completely finished: use up, eat up, drink up.

  5. The meeting kicked at nine sharp.

    Off can mean starting: kick off, set off.

  6. Once you start a project, follow it to the end.

    Through means from start to finish.

Phrasal verb or single verb?

Phrasal verbs are not informal mistakes. They are normal in conversation and in most emails. In formal reports, applications, and academic or legal writing, a single verb often sounds sharper. Strong writers move between the two depending on the reader.

Change the register

Rewrite each sentence for a formal report. Try it aloud first.

  1. We need to look into why sales went down.

    We need to investigate why sales declined.

  2. They’ve put off the decision until March.

    They have postponed the decision until March.

  3. The committee turned down our proposal.

    The committee rejected our proposal.

  4. We carried out a survey of 500 customers.

    We conducted a survey of 500 customers.

  5. She came up with a new pricing model.

    She devised a new pricing model.

  6. Now go the other way. Make this sound conversational: The event has been cancelled.

    The event has been called off.
