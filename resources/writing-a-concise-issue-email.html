The issue email

How to report a problem in English so it gets read, understood, and acted on — in as few words as it takes.

An issue email has one job: to get a problem understood and acted on, fast. The obstacle is rarely the reader's goodwill — it's their attention. Concision is how you protect it.

Concision is not brevity. It is making every word earn its place.

A concise issue email is read in one pass, understood without effort, and answered without a follow-up. This page shows how to write one.

§ 01

The anatomy

A good issue email is not shorter because it leaves things out — it is shorter because everything is in the right order. Six parts, top to bottom. Each one does a single job.

§ 02

The structural signature

Beyond the shape, a strong issue email carries a recognisable grammar. Name these before you write — they are your must-use structures. Aim to place each one deliberately, not by accident.

Past tense · what happened "The figures didn't arrive yesterday as agreed."
A passive · neutral report "The file was saved to the wrong folder." — when who did it doesn't matter.
An impact link · so what "As a result, the team can't prepare."
A concession · fairness "Although the deadline was tight, the delay wasn't flagged."
A modal request · the ask "Could you send them by end of day?"

Name the ones you'll use before you start, then tick them off after. It's how you know a draft is actually finished — not just long. A short, fixed list is easier to hit than a vague aim to "write well".

§ 03

Seven moves for concision

Concision is not a single act of cutting — it is a handful of repeatable decisions. Learn the moves and they become reflex.

Lead with the point, not the runway
Cut the throat-clearing opener. The first sentence is for the issue itself, not for arriving at it.
✗I am writing to inform you that there seems to be an issue with the report.
✓The report is missing two figures.
State the request early and plainly
Make the ask impossible to miss. Use a clear modal — and say who, what, and by when.
✗It would be greatly appreciated if the file could perhaps be sent at some point.
✓Could you send the file by 5pm today?
One idea per sentence
Long chains joined by "and… and… which… because…" bury the point. Split them so each lands.
✗The system crashed and we lost the data and now the client is waiting which is a problem.
✓The system crashed and we lost the data. The client is now waiting.
Turn nouns back into verbs
Nominal style is heavy. "Make a decision" is just "decide" wearing a coat. This is the single biggest win for French speakers.
✗We need to make a decision and provide confirmation of the change.
✓We need to decide and confirm the change.
Cut the empty words
"Just", "really", "very", "actually", "basically" dilute rather than strengthen. If the fact is urgent, the word "urgent" carries it.
✗I just wanted to say that it's actually really quite very urgent.
✓This is urgent.
Active for people, passive for process
Use the active voice to say what people do and to own a request. Keep the passive for when the doer is irrelevant — not to hide it.
✗A mistake was made and it will be looked into by someone.
✓We made a mistake. I'll look into it today. (But: "The order was cancelled" — fine, the who doesn't matter.)
One email, one purpose
Three unrelated problems in one wall of text get one partial answer. Send one issue — or list them clearly and numbered.
✗A paragraph that drifts from the printer to the parking to next week's agenda.
✓One issue per email — or a short numbered list if they truly belong together.
§ 04

The same email, twice

Same facts, same courtesy, same person sending it. The only difference is discipline. Read both aloud — the second one you can hear the reader answering.

Before ≈ 150 words
SubjectA small question

Dear Sir or Madam,

I hope this email finds you well. I am writing to you today because I wanted to inform you that I have noticed that there seems to be a bit of a problem regarding the monthly figures we were expecting for the review meeting on Thursday, which as you know is quite an important meeting for the whole team.

Basically, what I have observed is that the figures have not yet arrived in the shared folder, and this means it is not really possible for us to begin our preparation, and I think that maybe this could potentially become a problem as the meeting is approaching quite fast.

I would be very grateful if it would be possible for someone to maybe send them across when you have a moment. Thank you very much in advance for your kind attention to this matter.

Best regards,

After ≈ 60 words
SubjectMonthly figures still missing — blocking Thursday's review

Hi Marc,

The figures we agreed for Thursday's review haven't reached the shared folder yet.

Without them, the team can't start preparing — and the meeting is close.

Could you send them today, or let me know if there's a delay? Happy to help pull them together if that's useful.

Thanks,
Léa

What changed — and what didn't

  • The subject went from decorative to useful. "A small question" tells the reader nothing; the rewrite names the problem, the deadline, and the stakes.
  • The issue moved to sentence one. Three lines of "I hope… I am writing… I wanted to inform you" were runway, not message.
  • The impact became concrete. "Could potentially become a problem" is a shrug; "the team can't start preparing" is a reason to act.
  • The request became a request. "If it would be possible for someone to maybe send them" is a wish. "Could you send them today?" is answerable.
  • The courtesy stayed. Concise is not cold — the offer to help and the thanks are still there. It's warmth without padding.
§ 05

A phrase bank

Reusable, register-neutral building blocks — professional without being stiff. Pick one from each row you need and the email almost writes itself.

Open · state the issue

  • I want to flag an issue with…
  • There's a problem with…
  • [X] hasn't arrived / didn't happen.
  • Quick heads-up: …

Give the context

  • This happened when…
  • As of this morning, …
  • The report shows that…
  • To give you the background: …

State the impact

  • As a result, …
  • This means (that)…
  • The knock-on effect is…
  • If this isn't resolved, …

Concede & contrast

  • Although / Even though…, …
  • While I understand…, we still need…
  • Despite the delay, …
  • …, whereas previously…

Make the request

  • Could you… by [time]?
  • Please could you…?
  • Would you be able to…?
  • I'd need… before…

Close

  • Happy to discuss.
  • Let me know if you need more detail.
  • Thanks in advance.
  • I'll follow up on Thursday if I don't hear back.
§ 06

Before you send

Sixty seconds of self-audit. If any answer is "no", the email isn't finished.

  • Is the issue in the first sentence? Not the third paragraph.
  • Is the request clear — who, what, by when? A reader should know exactly what to do.
  • Have I cut the runway? No "I am writing to inform you that…"
  • One idea per sentence? Read it aloud; if you run out of breath, split it.
  • Any nouns hiding verbs? "Make a decision" → "decide".
  • Any empty words to cut? Just, really, very, basically, actually.
  • Would a busy reader get it in one pass? If not, tighten once more.
Concision is a rule you eventually learn to break on purpose.
Choose your directness deliberately
At C1, softening is a tool, not a habit. Decide how direct to be by reading the relationship and the stakes — then commit.
›Direct: "This needs clearing today."
›Softer: "It would help to have this cleared today." — same fact, calibrated tone.
Use the passive as a diplomatic instrument
Sometimes the neutral report is the tactful choice — it reports the fault without pointing at the person.
›"You missed the deadline" → "The deadline was missed." — the second de-escalates.
Put the ask in the stressed position
English gives weight to the end of a sentence. Land the request where it will be remembered.
›"By Friday, if you could send it, that would be great."
›"Could you send it by Friday?" — the deadline holds the stress.
Signpost a multi-point issue
When two or three things genuinely belong together, number them and give each its own request. Structure is itself a form of concision.
›"Two things need a decision: (1) the vendor, and (2) the deadline. On (1)…"
