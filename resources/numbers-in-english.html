Dates

The same date can be written three different ways — and the way it's said doesn't match the numeral at all.

Writing dates

How the date above would appear in each style

US (month / day / year)MM/DD/YYYY09/07/2026
International / ISO (forms, data)YYYY-MM-DD2026-09-07
UK, long form—7 September 2026
US, long form—September 7, 2026

07/09 is two different dates depending on where you are. In the UK it's 7 September; in the US it's July 9th. When the date matters — flights, contracts, forms — write the month as a word: 7 Sept 2026.

Saying dates aloud

  • The day is said as an ordinal, even though it's usually written as a plain number: 7 → the seventh.
  • UK pattern: the [ordinal] of [month] → “the seventh of September”.
  • US pattern: [month] [ordinal] → “September seventh” (no “the … of”).
  • With a preposition: “on the 7th of September” / “on September 7th”.
  • With the year: “the seventh of September, twenty twenty-six”.

Days of the month

Dates run on ordinal numbers — first, second, third. The rule is mostly regular, with a handful of forms worth knowing by sight.

Add -th to most numbers. 1, 2 and 3 are irregular (first, second, third), and that irregularity carries into twenty-one, twenty-two, twenty-three and thirty-one. But 11, 12 and 13 stay -th even though they end in 1, 2, 3 — they're the exception to the exception.

Counting numbers

The building blocks — and the two places English quietly splits from most other languages.

How numbers build up

NumberIn words
21twenty-one (hyphenated)
100one hundred / a hundred
101one hundred and one (UK) · one hundred one (US)
1,000one thousand
1,250one thousand two hundred and fifty
1,000,000one million
1,000,000,000one billion

English groups thousands with a comma and marks decimals with a full stop: 1,250.75. That's the reverse of French, German, Spanish and many other languages, which write 1.250,75. Worth checking twice on anything written for an English-speaking reader.

Numbers read digit by digit

  • Phone and reference numbers are read digit by digit, not as whole numbers: 020 7946 0958 → “oh two oh, seven nine four six, oh nine five eight”.
  • Repeated digits (British English): 55 → “double five”; 007 → “double oh seven”.

Position & order

Away from dates, ordinal numbers do the everyday work of describing order and place.

  • “the fourth house on the street”
  • “she lives on the third floor”
  • “he came second in the race”
  • “this is my second language”
  • “take the first left, then the second right”
  • “it's her 30th birthday” · “their 25th wedding anniversary”
  • “firstly … secondly … finally” — organising an explanation or an argument

Floors aren't counted the same everywhere. In the UK, the ground floor is floor 0, so the third floor is three storeys up. In the US, the ground floor is already the first floor, so the third floor is only two storeys up. Worth a second look on any room number.

Century numbers run one ahead of the years inside them: the 21st century covers 2001–2100, not the 2100s. A date in the 2020s belongs to “the 21st century”.

Money

Money combines everything above — whole numbers, decimals and separators — in one place.

Writing money

  • The symbol goes before the number, with no space: $50, £50, €50.
  • Decimals mark the smaller unit: $19.99, £4.50.
  • Thousands still take a comma: $1,250,000.
  • Reports and headlines abbreviate: $2.1m, £4.5bn, “a 12% rise”.

Talking about amounts approximately

  • just under $50 · just over £100
  • roughly €200 · in the region of $10,000
  • nearly a million

Data & statistics

The same rules power percentages, decimals and the numbers in any report or article.

Describing change

  • “sales rose by 20% to $2 million”
  • “the population increased from 1.2 to 1.5 million”
  • “prices fell by 8%” · “demand dropped to half its usual level”
  • “the figure doubled / halved / tripled”
  • “turnover remained stable at €4 million”

Big numbers in reports

  • 1.5 million people → “one point five million people”
  • 2.3 billion → “two point three billion”
  • headline shorthand: 2.1m, 4.5bn, 12k
