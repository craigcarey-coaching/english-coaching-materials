The past, in English

Three past tenses, the verbs that don't follow the rules, and the word pairs that French makes easy to mix up. Read it once with Craig, then come back to it whenever a sentence about yesterday doesn't feel right.

Work through the sections in order the first time. The practice exercises check themselves, and the speaking prompts at the end are for saying out loud, not writing.

1The big picture

English has three main ways to talk about the past. French mostly has two, which is where the trouble starts.

In French, j'ai signé (passé composé) covers almost every finished action. English splits that territory between the simple past and the present perfect, and the choice depends on one question: is the time finished, or does it connect to now? The past continuous is closer to the French imparfait: it describes something in progress.

2Simple past

The storytelling tense. Finished actions at a finished time.

How to form it

FormStructureExample
positiveverb + -ed, or the irregular formWe played in Lyon. We went to Berlin.
negativedidn't + base verbWe didn't play in Lyon. We didn't go to Berlin.
questionDid + subject + base verbDid you play in Lyon? Where did you go?
short answerYes, I did. / No, I didn't.Did he send the rider? Yes, he did.
The most common mistake: two past forms in one verb

Did already carries the past. The main verb goes back to its base form.

✗Did you saw the email? I didn't went.
✓Did you see the email? I didn't go.

The verb be is different: it doesn't use did. Use was / were, wasn't / weren't, and invert for questions.

✗Did you be at the show? I didn't be tired.
✓Were you at the show? I wasn't tired.

Spelling the -ed ending

Verb ends in…RuleExamples
most verbsadd -edbook → booked, sign → signed

short verb, one vowel + one consonantdouble the consonantplan → planned, stop → stopped

Saying the -ed ending

The -ed is almost never a separate syllable. It has three sounds, and only one of them adds a syllable.

/t/

After voiceless sounds: p, k, s, sh, ch, f, x.

booked, mixed, stopped, finished, asked

/d/

After voiced sounds and vowels: everything else.

played, signed, called, confirmed, toured

/ɪd/

Only after t or d. This one adds a syllable.

recorded, started, needed, invited, negotiated

French speakers often do one of two things

They pronounce every -ed as an extra syllable (book-ed, sign-ed), or they drop it completely so the listener hears the present (I book the flight). Say bookt and signd: one syllable, with a clear final sound.

Time words that go with it

If one of these is in the sentence, the simple past is almost always the right choice: yesterday, last night, last week, last year, two days ago, in 2019, on Friday, at 10 pm, when I was younger, when we arrived, then, after that.

3Past continuous

The background tense. Something in progress at a moment in the past.

FormStructureExample
positivewas / were + verb-ingI was waiting backstage. They were setting up.
negativewasn't / weren't + verb-ingShe wasn't listening.
questionWas / Were + subject + verb-ingWhat were you doing at midnight?

Three main uses

An action interrupted by a shorter one. The long action takes the past continuous; the short action that interrupts it takes the simple past. Use when before the short action, and while before the long one.

I was checking the lights when the power went out.
While we were driving to the venue, the promoter called.

Setting the scene in a story. It paints the picture before the events happen.

It was raining, people were queuing outside, and the DJ was still looking for his USB stick.

Two actions happening at the same time.

While I was negotiating the fee, my assistant was booking the hotel.

The test

If you would say j'étais en train de… in French, the past continuous is probably right. If you would use the passé composé for a single event, use the simple past.

Verbs that don't take -ing

Verbs about states rather than actions stay in the simple past, even when the situation lasted a long time: know, want, need, like, love, hate, believe, understand, mean, own, seem, belong.

✗I was knowing the promoter. She was wanting a higher fee.
✓I knew the promoter. She wanted a higher fee.

4Simple past or present perfect?

The passé composé trap, and the one decision that matters most.

The present perfect is have / has + past participle: I've signed, she's released, we haven't seen, have you been? It looks like the passé composé, and that is exactly the problem. The form is similar, but the meaning is not the same.

The French trap
✗I have seen him yesterday.
✗We have played in Amsterdam last October.
✓I saw him yesterday. We played in Amsterdam last October.

In English, a finished time (yesterday, last October) and the present perfect cannot go together. Never.

The decision in one question

Ask yourself

Can I say, or does the listener know, when it happened, and is that time finished?
Yes → simple past. No, or the time period is still open → present perfect.

When to use the present perfect

Experience, with no time given. Your life up to now is the time period, and it isn't finished.

I've worked at ADE three times. Have you ever been to Tokyo?
But as soon as you give the time, switch: I went in 2023.

An unfinished time period. Today, this week, this year, so far are still open.

We've signed three new artists this year. (the year isn't over)
We signed three new artists last year. (the year is over)

A past action with a result that matters now.

I've lost my phone. (I don't have it now)
The label has sent the contract. (it's here, we can read it)

A situation that started in the past and continues now, with for and since.

I've managed her for four years. We've known each other since 2019.

The depuis trap

French uses the present with depuis. English cannot.

✗I work with this label since 2021. How long are you a manager?
✓I've worked with this label since 2021. How long have you been a manager?

For actions (not states), you will also hear the present perfect continuous: I've been working on this deal for weeks. Both are correct.

Signal words

Simple past

yesterday · last week / month / year · … ago · in 2020 · on Monday · at 9 o'clock · when I was… · when did…?

Present perfect

ever · never · already · yet · just · so far · recently · this week / year (unfinished) · for · since · how long…?

For works with both, and the meaning changes: I lived in Berlin for two years (I don't live there now). I've lived in Paris for two years (I still live there).

How a real conversation moves

English speakers often open with the present perfect (general, no time), then switch to the simple past as soon as details and times appear.

— Have you ever played Dekmantel?
— Yes, we have. We played there in 2022.
— How was it?
— Amazing. The crowd was incredible, but it rained the whole weekend.

5Irregular verbs

The ones you actually need, grouped by pattern so they're easier to remember.

Every English verb has three forms: the base (take), the past simple (took) and the past participle (taken), used after have. Regular verbs use -ed for both. Irregular verbs don't, but most of them fall into a few patterns. Learn them in groups, not alphabetically.

Watch the participle

With have, use the third column, not the second.

✗Have you went there? I've wrote the email. She's took the call.
✓Have you been there? I've written the email. She's taken the call.

Go has two participles: been (you went and came back) and gone (you went and are still there). He's been to Ibiza = experience. He's gone to Ibiza = he's there now.

When you're not sure

Ask: is there a new thing at the end? A decision, a deal, a plan, money, a mistake: make. Or is it a task you carry out? Work, paperwork, research, a job: do. And with general words like something, nothing, everything, what, it's always do.

7See, look or watch?

French splits these between voir and regarder. English uses three verbs, and the difference is about attention and movement.

see

see · saw · seen

Your eyes are open and something comes into view. You don't have to try. Also used for meeting people and for understanding.

I saw him at the festival.
I see what you mean.

look (at)

look · looked · looked

You choose to direct your eyes at something, usually still and usually for a short time. Needs at before the object.

Look at this contract.
I looked at the poster.

watch

watch · watched · watched

You pay attention over time to something that moves, changes or happens: a film, a match, a set, a person.

We watched the whole set.
Did you watch the match?

The regarder trap
✗I looked the TV last night. Look this photo.
✓I watched TV last night. Look at this photo.

Regarder la télé / un film / un match → watch. Regarder une photo / un document / quelqu'un (briefly) → look at.

The small differences that matter

Films. You see a film at the cinema (We saw the new Villeneuve film) and watch a film at home. Both are common for a film you've seen in general: Have you seen / watched it?

Live performances. I saw Peggy Gou play in 2019 (the experience). I watched her set from the side of the stage (paying attention the whole time). After see / watch someone, use the base verb or -ing: I saw her play / playing.

Look = seem. Without at, look + adjective describes how something appears: You look tired. The new artwork looks great.

Keep an eye on. Can you watch my bag for a minute? means surveiller. For people or things you take care of longer, use look after: She looked after the artists on tour.

Looking for. Chercher is look for: I'm looking for my headphones. Have you seen them?

The same logic for sound

hear (heard) is like see: it just arrives. listen to (listened) is like watch: you pay attention. I heard a noise. I listened to the new track three times.

9Speak it

Answer out loud, in full sentences. Aim for one minute each. The tools under each prompt are the structures to use.

  1. Tell me about the last show you went to. Where were you, what was happening around you, and what happened that you remember?

    past continuous for the scene · simple past for the events · when / while

  2. What have you done this week so far? What did you do yesterday?

    present perfect for the open week · simple past for yesterday

  3. Describe a problem on tour. What were you doing when you found out, and what did you do next?

    was / were + -ing · when · then, after that, in the end

  4. Talk about one of your artists. How long have you worked together, and how did you first meet?

    for / since + present perfect · simple past for the first meeting

  5. What's the best decision you've made in your career? When did you make it, and why?

    make a decision · made / have made · irregular verbs

  6. Which artists have you seen live more than once? Tell me about the first time you saw one of them.

    have seen (experience) · saw (the first time) · watched the set

  7. Tell me about a deal that didn't work. What did you do, what mistakes did you make, and what have you done differently since?

    do / make · didn't + base verb · since then + present perfect
