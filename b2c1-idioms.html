Idioms in everyday and working English

It’s not ideal, but let’s sleep on it and decide in the morning.

The idioms on this page are grouped by what they help you do: start things, manage time, disagree politely, talk about problems, money and people. Each one comes with its meaning, a natural example and, where it matters, a note on register or regional use.

How idioms work

An idiom is a fixed phrase whose meaning is more than the sum of its words. You don’t need hundreds of them. You need the ones people really use, and you need to know how they fit into a sentence.

  • The key words are fixed. It was a piece of cake, not incorrect: a slice of cake. Change one word and the idiom usually stops working.
  • Verbs and pronouns change as normal. keep someone in the loop becomes I’ll keep you in the loop or She kept us in the loop.
  • Register varies. Some idioms are fine in a meeting or an email (on the same page, the bottom line). Others belong in relaxed conversation (a piece of cake, under the weather). Each idiom below marked informal is best kept for friends and colleagues you know well.
  • Less is more. One idiom in the right place sounds natural. Five in one paragraph sounds like a textbook.
  • Listen for half-idioms. Fluent speakers often say only part of a well-known idiom and expect you to know the rest: Well, it’s back to the drawing board, I suppose, or You know what they say about the tip of the iceberg.

Starting and making progress

get the ball rolling

start an activity or process

Shall I get the ball rolling with a quick update?

hit the ground running

start a new job or project quickly and effectively

We need someone who can hit the ground running.

learn the ropes

learn how a job or system works

It took me a few weeks to learn the ropes.

bring someone up to speed

give someone the latest information

Can you bring me up to speed on the Berlin project?

back to the drawing board

start planning again because the first idea failed

The client hated the design, so it’s back to the drawing board.

go the extra mile

make more effort than is expected

She always goes the extra mile for her customers.

cut corners

do something badly or cheaply to save time or money

If we cut corners on testing, we’ll pay for it later.

pull your weight

do your fair share of the work

Everyone on the team needs to pull their weight.

Check yourself

  1. I’m new here, so I’m still blank.

    Learn the ropes means learning how things work in a new job or place.

  2. The prototype failed every test. It’s blank.

    When a plan fails and you must start again, it’s back to the drawing board.

  3. Two people on the team aren’t blank, so everyone else is doing extra work.

    Pull your weight means doing your fair share.

  4. I was away for two weeks. Could someone blank?

    Bring someone up to speed means giving them the latest information.

Time and priorities

on the back burner

postponed; not a priority for now

The website redesign is on the back burner until spring.

in the pipeline

being planned or developed

We’ve got two new models in the pipeline.

at the eleventh hour

at the last possible moment

The deal was signed at the eleventh hour.

against the clock

with very little time to finish

We’re working against the clock to meet Friday’s deadline.

in the nick of time

informal

just before it was too late

I got to the gate in the nick of time.

ahead of the curve

more advanced than competitors or the general trend

They were ahead of the curve on electric vehicles.

call it a day

informal

stop working for the day

It’s nearly seven. Let’s call it a day.

Check yourself

  1. We’ve put the training programme blank while we deal with the merger.

    Something postponed and not a priority is on the back burner.

  2. The visa arrived blank, two days before my flight.

    In the nick of time means just before it was too late.

  3. Three new products are blank for next year.

    Things being planned or developed are in the pipeline.

  4. It’s been a long day. Let’s blank.

    The fixed form is call it a day.

Agreeing and disagreeing

see eye to eye

agree; most often used in the negative

We don’t always see eye to eye, but we respect each other.

on the same page

sharing the same understanding of a plan

Before we start, let’s make sure we’re all on the same page.

sit on the fence

avoid choosing a side

You can’t sit on the fence forever. Which option do you prefer?

play devil’s advocate

argue the opposite view to test an idea

Just to play devil’s advocate, what if prices fall next year?

agree to disagree

accept that you have different opinions and stop arguing

I think we’ll have to agree to disagree on that one.

beat around the bush

informal

avoid saying something directly

Don’t beat around the bush. Is the project cancelled or not?

In UK and Irish English, also: beat about the bush.

a bone of contention

a subject people keep disagreeing about

The budget has been a bone of contention for months.

Check yourself

  1. I don’t actually believe this, but let me blank for a moment.

    You play devil’s advocate when you argue a view you don’t hold, to test an idea.

  2. Parking spaces have always been blank between the two departments.

    A topic people keep arguing about is a bone of contention.

  3. You still haven’t told us what you think. Stop blank.

    Sitting on the fence means refusing to choose a side.

  4. My manager and I rarely blank on strategy.

    The fixed form is see eye to eye.

Problems and difficulties

a steep learning curve

a lot to learn in a short time

Moving into sales was a steep learning curve.

the tip of the iceberg

a small, visible part of a much bigger problem

These complaints are only the tip of the iceberg.

open a can of worms

start something that causes many new problems

Changing the contracts now would open a can of worms.

an uphill battle

a struggle that is very hard to win

Getting funding has been an uphill battle.

back to square one

back at the start, with no progress

The supplier pulled out, so we’re back to square one.

between a rock and a hard place

facing two equally bad choices

We’re between a rock and a hard place: raise prices or lose money.

a blessing in disguise

something bad that turns out to be good

Losing that job was a blessing in disguise.

Check yourself

  1. We fixed that bug, but it was only blank. There are dozens more.

    A small visible part of a bigger problem is the tip of the iceberg.

  2. If we start questioning the old policy, we’ll blank.

    Open a can of worms means creating a lot of new problems.

  3. Missing that flight was blank. I met my future business partner at the airport hotel.

    Something bad that turns out well is a blessing in disguise.

  4. Changing the culture of a large company is blank.

    A long, difficult struggle is an uphill battle.

Money and business

cost an arm and a leg

informal

be very expensive

Rent in the city centre costs an arm and a leg.

a ballpark figure

a rough estimate

Can you give me a ballpark figure for the repairs?

the bottom line

the most important fact; also, final profit or loss

The bottom line is that we can’t afford it.

cut your losses

stop something that is failing before it gets worse

Sales are still falling. It’s time to cut our losses.

tighten your belt

spend less money

With costs rising, we all need to tighten our belts.

in the red / in the black

losing money / making a profit

The company was in the red for three years before moving into the black.

Check yourself

  1. I don’t need exact numbers. Just give me blank.

    A rough estimate is a ballpark figure.

  2. The repairs blank. We can’t afford them this year.

    The fixed form is cost an arm and a leg.

  3. After two difficult years, the business is finally blank.

    In the black means making a profit; in the red means losing money.

  4. The project is failing and won’t recover. We should blank.

    Cut your losses means stopping before you lose even more.

People and relationships

break the ice

help people relax when they first meet

A quick question about the weekend can help break the ice.

get on like a house on fire

informal

like each other immediately

Our two teams got on like a house on fire.

keep someone in the loop

keep someone informed

Please keep me in the loop about any changes.

give someone the benefit of the doubt

believe someone even though you aren’t sure

He’s late again, but let’s give him the benefit of the doubt.

rub someone up the wrong way

informal

annoy someone, often without meaning to

His tone rubs people up the wrong way.

In US English: rub someone the wrong way.

on thin ice

informal

in a risky position, where one more mistake will cause trouble

After missing two deadlines, he’s on thin ice.

Check yourself

  1. The new manager is very direct, and it blank.

    Annoying people, often without meaning to, is rubbing them up the wrong way.

  2. I’m not sure his story is true, but I’ll blank.

    When you choose to believe someone despite doubts, you give them the benefit of the doubt.

  3. We started the workshop with a game to blank.

    The fixed form is break the ice.

  4. Please blank. I want to hear about any delays.

    The fixed form is keep someone in the loop.

Everyday conversation

not my cup of tea

informal

not something I enjoy

Opera isn’t really my cup of tea.

take something with a pinch of salt

not completely believe something

I’d take those figures with a pinch of salt.

In US English: a grain of salt.

under the weather

informal

slightly ill

I’m feeling a bit under the weather today.

ring a bell

informal

sound familiar

Her name rings a bell, but I can’t place her.

sleep on it

informal

wait until tomorrow before deciding

Don’t decide now. Sleep on it.

the penny dropped

informal

someone finally understood

I read the email three times, and then the penny dropped.

Mainly UK and Irish English.

a piece of cake

informal

very easy

The interview was a piece of cake.

Check yourself

  1. His name blank. Did he use to work here?

    The fixed form is ring a bell.

  2. I’d take that review blank. The writer works for a competitor.

    If you don’t fully believe something, you take it with a pinch of salt.

  3. Thanks for the offer. Can I blank and let you know tomorrow?

    The fixed form is sleep on it.

  4. Jazz isn’t really blank.

    Something you don’t enjoy is not your cup of tea.

Say it with an idiom

  1. Let’s stop working now. It’s late.

    Let’s call it a day.

  2. We need someone who can work effectively from the first day.

    We need someone who can hit the ground running.

  3. Can you give me a rough estimate?

    Can you give me a ballpark figure?

  4. We need to spend less this quarter.

    We need to tighten our belts this quarter.

  5. I don’t completely believe what he says.

    I take what he says with a pinch of salt.

  6. Let’s make sure we all understand the plan in the same way.

    Let’s make sure we’re all on the same page.

  7. Changing suppliers now would cause lots of new problems.

    Changing suppliers now would open a can of worms.
