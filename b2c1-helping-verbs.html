Helping verbs: can, could, would, should, do, did

‘You could have told me!’ ‘I did. You just weren’t listening, were you?’

Helping verbs do the quiet work of English grammar. They make questions and negatives, add meanings like possibility and obligation, add emphasis, and let you avoid repeating yourself. Fluent speakers use them constantly in short replies, tags and reactions, so they matter as much for listening as for speaking.

The first helping verb does the work

English has three main helping verbs (be, have, do) and the modals (can, could, will, would, should and the rest). One simple rule explains how they behave: the first helping verb in a sentence moves to make a question, takes not or n’t to make a negative, and comes back in short answers and question tags. If there is no helping verb, English brings in do, does or did.

StatementQuestionNegativeShort answer
She has been working late.Has she been working late?She hasn’t been working late.Yes, she has.
They will be there.Will they be there?They won’t be there.Yes, they will.
You should call him.Should I call him?You shouldn’t call him.Yes, you should.
He’s leaving.Is he leaving?He isn’t leaving.Yes, he is.
She works in finance.Does she work in finance?She doesn’t work in finance.Yes, she does.
They arrived late.Did they arrive late?They didn’t arrive late.Yes, they did.
Watch out. After do, does or did, the main verb goes back to its base form: Did they arrive?, not incorrect: Did they arrived?

The modals at a glance

Modals add meaning to the main verb: ability, permission, possibility, obligation, prediction. Most of them have more than one job, so context decides the meaning.

ModalMain jobsExamples
canability; permission; general possibility; informal requestsI can speak three languages. Can I park here? It can get very cold in January.
couldpast ability; polite requests; possibility; suggestionsCould you send me the file? It could rain later. You could try calling her.
mayformal permission; possibilityMay I ask a question? We may need more time.
mightpossibility (slightly less certain); gentle suggestionsI might be late. You might want to check that.
willprediction; decisions made now; willingness; offersIt’ll be fine. I’ll get it. The car won’t start.
wouldimagined situations; polite requests and offers; past habits; the future seen from the pastI would help if I could. Would you like a coffee? She said she’d call.
shalloffers and suggestions with I or weShall I open the window? Shall we start?
should / ought toadvice; what is expected or likelyYou should see a doctor. The parcel should arrive tomorrow.
muststrong obligation, often from the speaker; logical certaintyI must remember to call her. You must be tired.
have toobligation from outside (rules, circumstances)We have to wear a badge at work.
need to / needn’tnecessity; no necessityYou need to book in advance. You needn’t bring anything.
Regional note. In US English, shall is rare outside formal or legal writing. Americans usually say Should I…? or Do you want me to…?

Pairs that trip people up

could or was able to

For general ability in the past, both work. For one specific success, use was able to or managed to.

I could read when I was four. (general ability)

The fire spread quickly, but everyone was able to get out. (one success; incorrect: could get out sounds wrong)

In the negative, both are fine: I couldn’t / wasn’t able to open it.

mustn’t or don’t have to

In the positive, must and have to are close. In the negative they split completely.

You mustn’t smoke here. (it’s forbidden)

You don’t have to come if you’re busy. (it isn’t necessary)

would or used to

Both describe past habits. Only used to works for past states (things that were true for a long time).

We would / used to walk to school every day.

I used to live in Berlin. (incorrect: I would live in Berlin means something different: an imagined situation)

will or would in requests

Would adds distance, and distance sounds polite.

Will you close the door? (direct; can sound like an order)

Would you close the door? (softer)

should or had better

Should gives general advice. Had better adds a warning: something bad will happen if you don’t.

You should try the fish here.

You’d better leave now, or you’ll miss the train.

Sounding polite and diplomatic

Past forms such as could, would, might and was wondering create distance, and in English, distance sounds polite. That’s why I was wondering if… is about now, not the past. Here is one request, from most direct to most indirect.

  • instruction
    Send me the report.
  • friendly, informal
    Can you send me the report?
  • polite, safe default
    Could you send me the report?
  • polite
    Would you mind sending me the report? (followed by -ing)
  • formal
    Would it be possible to send me the report?
  • very polite, tentative
    I was wondering if you could send me the report.

Softening advice, suggestions and disagreement

  • You might want to double-check the numbers. (= I think you should)
  • It might be worth asking IT.
  • I’d suggest waiting until Monday.
  • Shall we take a short break?
  • Could we look at this from another angle?
  • I wouldn’t say it was a failure, exactly.
  • That would be great. I would think so.
Watch out. ‘Would you mind waiting?’ ‘No, not at all.’ means yes, I’ll wait. You are saying you don’t mind.

Check yourself

  1. ‘Would you mind waiting a moment?’ You’re happy to wait. You say: blank

    Not at all means I don’t mind, so yes, I’ll wait.

  2. Make this more polite: Give me a lift to the station.

    Could you give me a lift to the station? / Would you mind giving me a lift to the station?

  3. Make this more polite: Move your car.

    Would you mind moving your car?

  4. Soften this advice: Check the figures again.

    You might want to check the figures again.

  5. Make this very polite: Call me back today.

    I was wondering if you could call me back today.

  6. Soften this disagreement: Your idea won’t work.

    I’m not sure that would work. / I wonder if that might be difficult.

Guessing and deducing

Modals let you say how sure you are. Use the base verb for the present and have + past participle for the past.

How sure?About nowAbout the past
almost certainly yesShe must be at lunch.She must have left already.
possiblyHe might be / may be / could be stuck in traffic.He might have missed the train.
almost certainly notThat can’t be right.They can’t have received it yet.
Watch out. The opposite of must for certainty is can’t, not mustn’t: It can’t be true, not incorrect: It mustn’t be true.
Continuous forms. She must be working late. He might have been sleeping when you called.

Check yourself

  1. All the lights are off. They blank gone to bed.

    The evidence makes you almost certain: must have.

  2. That blank be Anna. She’s in Madrid this week.

    Almost certainly not: can’t.

  3. I’m not sure where my keys are. I blank left them at work.

    You’re not sure, so might have or may have.

  4. You blank be exhausted after that flight.

    A logical conclusion: must.

  5. He blank seen the message. His phone was off all day.

    Almost certainly not, in the past: can’t have.

Looking back: should have, could have, would have

should have + past participle

regret or criticism: the right thing didn’t happen

I should have booked earlier.

You shouldn’t have said that.

could have + past participle

a possibility that didn’t happen; mild criticism

We could have won.

You could have told me!

would have + past participle

the imagined result of a different past

If I’d known, I would have helped.

needn’t have or didn’t need to

needn’t have = you did it, but it wasn’t necessary. didn’t need to = it wasn’t necessary (usually you didn’t do it).

I needn’t have taken a coat. It was warm.

I didn’t need to take a coat, so I left it at home.

Listening tip. In fast speech, have shrinks to a weak sound. Should have sounds like ‘shoulda’ or ‘should’ve’, could have like ‘coulda’, would have like ‘woulda’. That’s why people sometimes write incorrect: should of. The correct written forms are should have and should’ve.

Check yourself

  1. I’m starving. I blank breakfast.

    Regret about the past: should have.

  2. Why didn’t you ask me? I blank you.

    The imagined result of a different past: would have.

  3. We blank a taxi. The hotel was only two minutes’ walk away.

    You did it, but it wasn’t necessary: needn’t have.

  4. Be careful on those stairs! You blank fallen.

    A possibility that didn’t happen: could have.

  5. If I’d left earlier, I blank the train.

    Third conditional: if + had + past participle, would have + past participle.

  6. People sometimes write ‘should of’. What is the correct form?

    Should have, or should’ve in informal writing.

Do and did for emphasis

In positive sentences, do, does and did can add emphasis. In speech, they are always stressed.

  • Insisting: I did send it. Check your spam folder.
  • Contrasting: I don’t eat meat, but I do eat fish.
  • Correcting: ‘You never call.’ ‘I do call! I called on Sunday.’
  • Admitting a point: It does look expensive, but it lasts for years.
  • Warm invitations: Do sit down. Do let me know if you need anything.
Other tenses. This only works with the present simple, the past simple and imperatives. With other tenses, stress the helping verb that’s already there: I have finished! She is coming.

Check yourself

  1. ‘You didn’t lock the door.’ ‘I blank lock it! I remember doing it.’

    Insisting about the past: did + base verb.

  2. I don’t usually like big cities, but I like Barcelona.

    Contrast in the present simple: do + base verb.

  3. It blank seem strange, but it’s true.

    It takes does, followed by the base verb seem.

  4. ‘You haven’t finished.’ ‘I blank finished! Look.’

    The present perfect already has a helping verb, so stress have.

Short replies and reactions

In real conversation, helping verbs carry most of the short replies. A bare yes or no can sound abrupt; adding the helping verb sounds more natural and engaged.

FunctionExamples
Short answers‘Are you ready?’ ‘Yes, I am.’ ‘Has she called?’ ‘No, she hasn’t.’
Showing interest or surprise‘I’m moving to Lisbon.’ ‘Are you? That’s exciting!’ ‘She quit yesterday.’ ‘Did she? Why?’
Agreeing (positive)‘I love this song.’ ‘So do I.’ ‘I’m exhausted.’ ‘So am I.’
Agreeing (negative)‘I can’t swim.’ ‘Neither can I.’ ‘I didn’t get the email.’ ‘Nor did I.’
Disagreeing politely‘I love this weather.’ ‘Do you? I don’t.’ ‘I’m not tired.’ ‘I am!’
Informal alternatives. Me too and me neither are fine in casual speech.
Regional note. Irish speakers very often answer with the helping verb alone, without yes or no: ‘Did you get the tickets?’ ‘I did.’ ‘Are you coming tonight?’ ‘I am, yeah.’ This comes from the Irish language, which has no single words for yes and no.

Check yourself

  1. ‘I’ve never been to Japan.’ (You haven’t either.) blank

    Negative agreement: Neither + helping verb + subject.

  2. ‘I’m starving.’ (You are too.) blank

    The statement uses am, so the reply uses am.

  3. ‘We’re getting a dog.’ ‘blank What kind?’

    An echo question repeats the helping verb from the statement: are.

  4. ‘She speaks four languages.’ (You’re surprised.) blank

    Speaks is present simple, so the echo uses does.

  5. ‘I didn’t enjoy the film.’ (You didn’t either.) blank

    Negative agreement: Nor + helping verb + subject.

Question tags

  • Positive statement, negative tag. Negative statement, positive tag. It’s cold, isn’t it? You haven’t met, have you?
  • Use the first helping verb, or do, does or did if there isn’t one. She works here, doesn’t she? They left early, didn’t they?
  • I am becomes aren’t I. I’m late, aren’t I?
  • Let’s becomes shall we. Let’s go, shall we?
  • Instructions take will you, won’t you or could you. Pass the salt, could you?
  • Never, nobody, nothing and hardly count as negative. You never call, do you? Nobody noticed, did they?
  • Nothing and everything become it; nobody and everyone become they. Everything’s fine, isn’t it? Everyone was there, weren’t they?
  • There is keeps there. There’s a problem, isn’t there?
Listening tip. Intonation changes the meaning. With a falling tag, you expect the other person to agree; it’s really a comment: Nice day, isn’t it? With a rising tag, you genuinely don’t know and want an answer: You’re from Leeds, aren’t you?

  2. She hasn’t called yet, ?

    Negative statement with has, so has she?

  3. They moved to Galway last year, ?

    Past simple, no helping verb, so didn’t they?

  4. Let’s order pizza, ?

    Let’s always takes shall we?

  5. Nobody saw anything, ?

    Nobody is negative, so the tag is positive, with they.

  6. I’m on the list, ?

  7. You can drive, ?

    Positive statement with can, so can’t you?

  8. There isn’t much time left, ?

Saying less: leaving words out

Fluent speakers avoid repeating a verb that everyone already understands. Stopping at the helping verb (or at to) sounds natural. Repeating everything sounds heavy.

TechniqueExamples
Stop at the helping verbI’d like to help, but I can’t. She said she’d finish it, and she did. ‘You should apologise.’ ‘I know I should.’
Stop at to‘Are you coming?’ ‘I’d love to.’ I didn’t want to go, but I had to.
Replace a clause with so or not‘Is it going to rain?’ ‘I hope not.’ ‘Will they agree?’ ‘I think so.’ ‘Is it serious?’ ‘I’m afraid so.’
If so, if notCheck whether the room is free. If so, book it. If not, we’ll use my office.
Do so (formal)He promised to reply, but he never did so.
Natural choice. I don’t think so is far more common than I think not, which sounds formal or old-fashioned.

Check yourself

  1. ‘Can you come to the party?’ ‘I’d love blank, but I’m away that weekend.’

    Stop at to: I’d love to.

  2. ‘Will the shop be open on Sunday?’ ‘I don’t blank.’

    I don’t think so replaces the whole clause.

  3. I wasn’t going to apply, but in the end I blank.

    Stop at the helping verb: I did.

  4. ‘Is it serious?’ ‘I’m afraid blank.’ (= yes, unfortunately)

    I’m afraid so means yes; I’m afraid not means no.

  5. Most people have finished, but Tom blank.

    Stop at the helping verb from the first clause: have becomes hasn’t.
